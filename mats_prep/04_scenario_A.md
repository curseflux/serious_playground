## Document 3 — Email from the post-training team

> **Subject: Re: rc3 delta — what changed**
>
> Between rc2 and rc3 the substantive changes were:
>
> 1. New constitutional data source added to the RL mix, roughly 2% of total. It targets
>    **overclaiming** — the model asserting things with more confidence than warranted.
>    Generated by the usual pipeline; not human-reviewed beyond spot checks.
> 2. Routine data refresh on the SFT mix (~0.5% change in composition).
> 3. One hyperparameter change in the final RL stage (KL coefficient reduced).
>
> Nothing was targeted at refusal behaviour. We'd be surprised if (1) moved benign refusal
> by five points, though we haven't looked at the transcripts ourselves.
>
> Re-running the full RL stage with (1) removed would take about 6 days of compute.

Things a strong answer would probably do. This is not an answer key — there genuinely
isn't one — but if you missed several of these, that's where to look:

- **Identify the harness confound as the dominant one.** The rc3 run was the first on the
  new harness (Doc 2), the harness touched system prompts (Doc 2), and the external
  evaluator using a *different* system prompt sees no regression (Doc 4). That's three
  independent pointers at the same confound.
- **Propose re-running rc2 on the new harness as the top action.** ~6 hours, ~$40k, within
  your authority, and it cleanly separates "the model changed" from "the measurement
  changed." Recognising that one cheap move dominates is the main taste signal in this
  scenario.
- **Notice the long-context drop is diagnostic, not a side quest.** Nothing in the
  training changes plausibly explains a 4-point long-context drop *and* a refusal jump.
  A single common cause upstream (the harness) explains both. Facts that look unrelated
  are often the ones that discriminate between hypotheses.
- **Notice that the customer ticket cuts the other way.** Doc 5 is a real user on the
  real endpoint hitting real over-refusal on exactly the content type ("conflict",
  "threat") that a badly-generalizing safety-adjacent data source would catch. This is
  evidence that *something* real is happening in the model, independent of the harness.
  Strong answers hold both: the harness likely inflates the *measured* delta, and there
  may still be a real, smaller effect underneath. Weak answers pick one and drop the other.
- **Handle the eval-awareness observation proportionately.** Doc 2's ten transcripts are
  the most alarming item and the weakest evidence in the packet: no baseline, n≈10,
  self-reported, and the observer changed his own prompting mid-stream. A good answer
  says it's low-prior *and* worth a cheap systematic check *and* explains why the cost
  asymmetry justifies spending something on it. It neither ignores it nor builds the
  answer around it.
- **Question whether the deltas are real at all.** No confidence intervals anywhere. The
  long-context n is 150. Ask for CIs — it's free.
- **Read the transcripts.** 148 newly-refused prompts exist; someone should read them and
  categorize. Cheap, fast, and it directly tests the "overclaiming data source
  over-generalized" hypothesis: do the refusals cluster on uncertainty-laden or
  conflict-laden content?
- **Get the external evaluator's system prompt** (Doc 4 Appendix B). One email. If their
  system prompt differs from the internal harness default in a relevant way, that closes
  the loop.
- **Engage the actual tradeoff.** The 6-day RL re-run consumes the entire buffer and
  leaves no time to evaluate the result — proposing it without acknowledging that it
  eliminates your ability to check the fix is a trap worth spotting.
- **Recommend something, with a trigger.** E.g. don't hold the launch *yet*; re-run rc2
  on the new harness tonight; escalate if the delta reproduces. And note that the
  4-day human preference round must be requested *tomorrow* — a decision with a deadline
  earlier than the launch, which is easy to miss.
- **Note the disclosure question.** Doc 4 is an external evaluator whose findings
  disagree with yours. There's a straightforward professional obligation to tell them
  what you've found and why you think the results differ. Strong answers mention it.
